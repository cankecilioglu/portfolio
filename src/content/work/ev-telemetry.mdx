A battery current reading is two measurements in one sign. Positive means the
pack is supplying power; negative means the car is recovering energy while it
brakes or coasts. That single signed number is what a range estimate, a thermal
model or an energy dashboard actually needs, and it is what this project
estimates from signals available **at the same instant**: speed, acceleration,
pedal travel, road grade, and the ECO / air-conditioning states.

It began as the term project for *Python for Engineers* at the Turkish-German
University. The brief was open-ended, so I treated it as a small engineering
study rather than a plot collection: clean the data honestly, state a physical
model with its assumptions, compare it with a learned model and a mean baseline,
and analyse where each one fails.

## The question, stated precisely

Can `CUR` — battery current in ampere, signed, including regenerative braking —
be estimated from simultaneous vehicle signals only? Not forecast: every feature
is measured at the same timestamp as the target. The test set is the **latest
20 % of the recorded time**, so the score measures performance on unseen drivers
and an unseen day.

That choice matters. A random split would let the model memorise the route and
the driver, and it would look far better than it is.

## The data are not clean, and that is part of the story

The dataset is public: nine trips over the same ~76 km route in Brazil, October
2023, 24,277 rows at roughly 2 s sampling. It is also messier than its
description suggests:

- **The file is shuffled.** Rows from all trips are interleaved; sorting per trip is the first operation.
- **97 timestamps cannot be trusted.** Impossible components (month 49, hour 83) or stamps contradicting the recording order are dropped, because there is no honest way to place them in time.
- **Corrupt channels are flagged, not deleted.** Pack voltage is outside 250–420 V on 18,049 rows; altitude has four zero sentinels; 21 GPS fixes fall outside Brazil. Those rows still carry valid speed and current, so they stay, with `vol_valid`, `alt_missing` and `gps_valid` flags.
- **The accelerometer is unusable as acceleration.** `AX` has a per-trip offset between −0.1 and +3.1 m/s² and correlates with differentiated speed at only r = 0.14. The pipeline uses `dSPD/dt` instead, and documents why.

The cleaning report is generated, not hand-written: every count in the project
comes from the same code path that produced the figures.

## A physical model first

The reference model is a longitudinal power balance at the pack:

```text
I = [ m·a·v + ½·ρ·C_d·A·v³ + m·g·sin(θ)·v + C_rr·m·g·v ] / (η·V_nom)
    + (P_base + P_air·AIR) / V_nom
```

Dividing by the nominal pack voltage makes the equation **linear in six
coefficients** — apparent mass, aerodynamic drag, grade force, rolling
resistance, base load and air-conditioning load — so it can be calibrated by
ordinary least squares on the training rows alone. Grade comes from altitude and
odometer over a trailing ~30 s window; it is causal, not a centred smoothing.

The fitted coefficients are converted back into engineering quantities, which is
where the model earns or loses trust. Two independent estimates of vehicle mass
— one from traction, one from grade — agree within 10 % (873 kg and 959 kg). Both
sit about 40 % below the published 1,535 kg kerb mass, most plausibly because a
2 s finite difference smooths acceleration peaks and one efficiency cannot
describe traction and regeneration at once. That is a reported limitation, not a
number I tuned away.

## Then a learned model, and a baseline that keeps everyone honest

Four methods are scored on the same held-out rows. The Random Forest is tuned
with time-series cross-validation **inside the training set**; no test row
influences the imputer or the hyper-parameters. The optional linear regression
on speed and acceleration is the simple reference the brief allowed.

| Model | Test MAE | RMSE | Bias | R² |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Training mean (baseline) | 40.35 A | 54.58 | −2.84 | −0.003 |
| Linear: speed + acceleration | 31.96 A | 43.94 | −2.62 | 0.350 |
| Calibrated physics | 25.03 A | 36.05 | −0.47 | 0.562 |
| Random Forest | **5.43 A** | 9.43 | −0.47 | 0.970 |

The gap has a clear cause. The accelerator pedal alone correlates with current
at r = +0.91, and 90 % of the forest's feature importance lands on `ACC`. The
forest is, to a large extent, a learned pedal-to-current map of this particular
vehicle. That is a legitimate simultaneous signal, but it is also why the
physics model is the one that would transfer to another EV, while the forest is
tied to this pedal calibration.

## Where the errors live

<Figure
  src={img('error-analysis.png')}
  alt="Two panels: MAE by speed band grows for baseline, linear and physics but stays low for random forest; MAE by operating regime is lowest while braking"
  kind="error analysis"
  ratio="16 / 9"
  caption="Residuals on the test set. The mean baseline is badly biased above 80 km/h; the physics error grows with speed; the forest stays between 2.6 and 7.4 A in every speed band."
/>

The pattern is informative rather than decorative:

- The **baseline** has a −36 A bias above 80 km/h and −29 A uphill. It predicts
  the average of a mostly low-demand dataset and cannot follow demand.
- The **physics model** is best while braking (14.6 A), where the recovery term
  is proportional to speed and dominates, and worst above 80 km/h (39–45 A),
  where aerodynamic and efficiency assumptions matter most.
- The **forest** stays between 3.9 A (braking) and 6.5 A (coasting) per regime.
  Its largest remaining bias is about −3.5 A at 80–100 km/h.

A second split holds out the last 20 % of *every* trip, putting all nine drivers
in the test set. The ranking does not change (forest 6.2 A, physics 27.2 A,
linear 33.2 A, baseline 38.5 A), so the result is not an artefact of which trips
happen to fall after the global cut.

## The ECO-mode trap

Trip 4 switches economy mode on and off four times: same car, same route, same
driver. The raw numbers look like a clean result — and they are wrong. ECO-on
windows also contain twice the accelerator pedal (27 % versus 12 %) and sit on
slightly uphill sections (+2.2 % versus −1.2 % grade). Raw current is therefore
*higher* with ECO on: 49.5 A versus 19.7 A.

<Figure
  src={img('eco-matched.png')}
  alt="Left: current trace of trip 4 with shaded ECO-active windows. Right: bar chart of current change at matched speed and pedal, between minus 34 and minus 16 amperes in every speed band"
  kind="ECO experiment"
  ratio="16 / 9"
  caption="Raw trace on the left; matched comparison on the right. Holding speed band and accelerator-pedal quintile fixed reverses the sign: ECO-on current is 16–34 A lower, about −20 A overall."
/>

Comparing only samples in the same speed band **and** the same pedal quintile
reverses the sign: ECO-on is 16–34 A lower in every band, with a matched average
of −20 A (a regression that also controls for grade gives ≈ −15 A). The mode
does save current; the raw comparison was measuring the driver's right foot.

That reframing is the part of the project I would keep in any future analysis.
Observational data rarely gives you a clean experiment, and matching is often
the difference between a finding and a plausible-looking artefact.

## What I would change next

- **More drivers and more days.** The global test set is two drivers on one day;
  the per-trip check mitigates but does not remove that limitation.
- **Separate charge and discharge efficiencies**, and a shorter acceleration
  window, to see whether the fitted mass moves toward the published kerb mass.
- **A transfer test.** Train the pedal map on one vehicle and apply it to
  another; my expectation is that the physics model degrades gracefully and the
  forest does not.
- **A proper state-of-charge model.** Current integrates to charge, so the
  natural next step is a small battery model rather than a single-step estimate.
